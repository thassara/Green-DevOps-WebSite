Context

Research background

Every build, test, deployment and running workload consumes computing resources. Conventional DevOps improves delivery speed and reliability, but these decisions do not routinely account for energy consumption or the changing carbon intensity of electricity.

Green DevOps extends this operational perspective to environmental sustainability. The research considers both how much work is necessary and when that work should run, while retaining delivery, performance and service-level requirements.

The project follows four stages because sustainability decisions continue after a successful build: a deployment has its own resource footprint, runtime demand changes, and monitoring is needed to identify regressions and inform the next decision.

Source: revised manuscript, Introduction and Research Framework Overview. The development of this approach is documented in the research milestones.

Research foundations

Literature survey

The journal connects five areas of prior work. Together they establish the methods used by the project and explain why coordination across stages matters.

Sustainable software engineering and Green DevOps

The Karlskrona Manifesto treats sustainability as a concern that spans a system and its wider effects. GREENSOFT introduces a lifecycle perspective for green software assessment. Green DevOps and SusDevOps research extend these principles into delivery processes, resource use and organizational decision-making. [47] [48] [49] [50]

Relevance: environmental impact becomes an ongoing software engineering consideration, alongside operational quality.

Energy measurement, carbon accounting and observability

Direct hardware measurements are difficult to obtain in virtualized infrastructure. Resource-based energy models provide an alternative, with uncertainty. The Software Carbon Intensity specification and Cloud Carbon Footprint methodology establish approaches to environmental accounting. Monitoring needs historical context as well as current measurements. [6] [53] [60]

Relevance: the project estimates energy and carbon from available telemetry, then builds stage-specific sustainability baselines.

Sustainability in CI/CD and selective execution

Regression test selection identifies tests affected by software changes. Related work distinguishes selection, minimization and prioritization; modular build tools support dependency-aware execution. These methods reduce repeated computation, but do not by themselves decide whether the current grid conditions are favorable. [1] [2] [3]

Relevance: Release combines change-impact analysis with a separate carbon-intensity decision.

Carbon-aware computing, scheduling and deployment

Carbon-aware computing uses electricity-grid information to guide workload timing or placement. Datacenter scheduling and bounded temporal shifting demonstrate why flexible work can benefit from cleaner execution windows. Deployment research such as FREEDA also considers infrastructure selection across cloud and edge environments. [4] [5] [25]

Relevance: the framework considers execution timing and deployment strategy while respecting urgency and reliability requirements.

AI-driven resource management and anomaly detection

Predictive resource management uses workload history to anticipate demand before reactive thresholds are crossed. At the monitoring layer, statistical methods offer interpretable deviations, while Isolation Forest detects unusual combinations of features without requiring labelled training anomalies. Extended Isolation Forest develops this approach further. [41] [57] [58] [59]

Relevance: Operation combines forecasts with carbon and priority decisions; Monitoring adds historical and multidimensional analysis.

The challenge

Research problem

Release

Rebuilding and retesting an entire repository for a small change repeats unnecessary work. Build timing also ignores the electricity grid’s changing carbon intensity.

Deployment

Immediate promotion after build completion can miss lower-carbon windows. Different deployment strategies also create different amounts of overlapping infrastructure.

Operation

Reactive scaling responds after utilization changes. It generally does not combine future demand, carbon estimates and the urgency of individual jobs.

Monitoring

Performance observability does not automatically reveal whether an execution is environmentally unusual compared with its own historical baseline.

Source: revised manuscript, Introduction → Problem Statement.

The integration gap

Four decisions, one lifecycle

The literature reviewed in the journal largely treats selective execution, carbon-aware timing, predictive scaling and sustainability monitoring as separate concerns.

The research gap is their coordination: build decisions need environmental context, deployment decisions need operational constraints, runtime decisions need forecasts and priorities, and subsequent executions need feedback from observed sustainability behavior.

The proposed framework connects these mechanisms across Release, Deployment, Operation and Monitoring. Its contribution is the consistent use of sustainability information across the lifecycle, rather than a claim that any individual algorithm is new.

Source: revised manuscript, Introduction → Research Gap and Novelty Statement; Related Work → Research Gap.

Final research questions

What the study asks

  1. RQ1 — Release

    To what extent can git-diff-based, module-level change-impact analysis, combined with a learned carbon-intensity classifier, reduce both unnecessary build/test execution and the carbon cost of necessary execution in CI/CD pipelines?

  2. RQ2 — Deploy

    How can real-time grid carbon-intensity signals and predictive analytics be incorporated into deployment scheduling to identify low-carbon deployment windows without degrading delivery performance?

  3. RQ3 — Operate

    How accurately can an LSTM-based model predict Kubernetes workload demand, and how can carbon-emission estimation and workload prioritization be integrated into scaling decisions to reduce environmental impact while preserving SLA compliance?

  4. RQ4 — Monitor

    How can sustainability monitoring be extended from measurement alone to historical baseline modelling and anomaly detection, enabling automatic identification of sustainability regressions across the Release, Deploy, and Operate stages?

Question text reproduced from the revised manuscript, Introduction → Research Questions.

Research direction

Objectives

Develop and evaluate an integrated framework that reduces unnecessary computation and supports carbon-aware decisions while preserving delivery velocity, application performance and SLA requirements.

  1. Release: develop and empirically benchmark a module-level change-impact mechanism and a carbon-intensity classifier for build/test scheduling.
  2. Deployment: establish fine-grained deployment-stage carbon measurement and use predictive analytics to autonomously identify low-carbon deployment windows.
  3. Operation: integrate LSTM workload forecasting, carbon-emission estimation and workload prioritization into a runtime decision engine for sustainable Kubernetes scaling.
  4. Monitoring: construct historical sustainability baselines and a hybrid anomaly-detection mechanism for regressions across Build, Test and Deploy activity.
  5. Integrated evaluation: assess reductions in unnecessary compute and carbon emissions across the lifecycle while preserving operational requirements, including potential cost and performance benefits.

Novelty and contribution

A connected decision framework

The framework connects four complementary mechanisms: an empirically benchmarked selective CI engine and carbon classifier; deployment-stage measurement and scheduling; predictive Kubernetes decision support with workload priorities; and historical sustainability analysis with anomaly detection.

Monitoring closes the loop by returning carbon-efficiency assessments, anomaly reports and a Sustainability Health Score to earlier decisions. This makes the integrated architecture the central research contribution.

Source: revised manuscript, Research Contributions and Sustainability Feedback Loop.

Proposed solution

Methodology

The method combines deterministic change analysis, learned predictions, software-based carbon estimation and constrained decision support. Each stage consumes information from the previous stages and produces evidence for monitoring.

  1. Collect the relevant inputs. Source diffs, module dependencies, deployment telemetry, runtime history, workload urgency and grid carbon-intensity information establish the decision context.
  2. Reduce unnecessary execution. Identify affected Maven modules, select build/test work and classify carbon conditions before scheduling eligible Release tasks.
  3. Coordinate deployment. Estimate deployment energy and emissions, then evaluate execution windows and Recreate, Rolling or Canary strategies against operating requirements.
  4. Anticipate runtime demand. Feed historical telemetry into an LSTM; combine its forecast with carbon estimates and job priorities to recommend a scaling or delay action.
  5. Learn from observed behavior. Store stage-level measurements, establish historical baselines, flag anomalies and return sustainability assessments to future decisions.
  6. Evaluate component evidence. Use time-series validation, paired Jenkins benchmarks, unseen workload traces and historical monitoring records. Keep measured outcomes separate from proposed operational benefits.

Carbon accounting: energy is estimated from resource utilization. Carbon calculations combine estimated energy, regional carbon intensity and infrastructure overhead such as Power Usage Effectiveness. These are model-based estimates, with uncertainty; they are not direct hardware power measurements.

Source: revised manuscript, Methodology; Implementation; Results and Evaluation.

Four research components

How each stage contributes

Each component addresses a distinct source of inefficiency. Their methods and evidence are presented separately so that implementation capabilities can be distinguished from experimentally measured results.

Component 01

Release management

Avoid unnecessary work, then consider the carbon conditions for work that remains.

Problem and objective. Whole-repository execution repeats computation for changes affecting only a small part of the project. Release aims to reduce that scope and support lower-carbon timing for suitable builds.

Approach and implementation

A Python package runs from a Jenkins shell step or GitHub Actions workflow. It maps changed files to their Maven modules using longest-prefix matching and expands the selected set through Maven’s -am reactor behavior. Documentation-only changes can bypass execution; shared dependencies and global build configuration trigger broader validation.

A LightGBM classifier uses 39 temporal features, including lag and rolling-window statistics, to classify carbon conditions. During high-carbon conditions, a Prophet forecaster searches for a cleaner hour within a maximum eight-hour delay. Eligible non-urgent builds shift when a better window exists; otherwise they proceed.

Method details and evaluation evidence

The supplied configuration compares HEAD~1 with HEAD, includes a shared common-lib module, and treats root Maven configuration as global triggers. Its example carbon provider is configured as a mock; this example should not be read as proof that every experiment used a live grid API.

Five-fold time-series cross-validation produced LightGBM macro F1 0.9787 and approximately 98% recall. Lag and rolling features contributed approximately 96.2% of SHAP feature importance. The supplementary Prophet model had approximately 51% MAPE, indicating that the forecasting layer needs refinement.

Ten paired optimized and unoptimized Jenkins runs on identical commits showed 47.7% mean wall-clock reduction and 90.4% of possible tests skipped. These are execution-efficiency results, not a measured carbon-reduction percentage.

Integration. Release passes optimized artifacts, build metadata and sustainability information to Deployment. Monitoring feedback can inform subsequent scheduling decisions.

Source: revised manuscript, Release Phase Methodology; Release Stage Implementation; CI/CD Optimization Performance. Algorithms: LightGBM [9], Prophet [13].

Component 02

Carbon-aware deployment

Consider deployment timing, infrastructure overlap and reliability together.

Problem and objective. A deployment scheduled immediately after a build may run during carbon-intensive periods. This component establishes fine-grained energy and carbon estimates and uses them to inform deployment windows and strategy choices.

Approach and implementation

Deployment CPU, memory and network telemetry supports software-based energy estimation. Grid carbon-intensity signals provide environmental context. The final implementation describes a ReAct agent—reasoning and acting—whose decision logic is framed as a Markov Decision Process.

The agent considers current grid conditions, system load and historical strategy performance when choosing among Recreate, Rolling and Canary updates. These strategies have different resource-overlap and availability implications. Kubernetes Node Affinity, Taints and Tolerations provide placement mechanisms.

Strategy reasoning and evaluation scope

The manuscript describes Canary as an option for limiting initial load spikes and Recreate for suitable low-carbon maintenance windows. These are context-dependent decisions: infrastructure efficiency is considered alongside operational stability and release requirements.

The component is described within the integrated implementation, and Deploy records form part of the monitoring experiment. The final Results section does not report a separate deployment benchmark, numerical emissions saving or quantified delivery-performance comparison.

Integration. Deployment consumes successful Release outputs and passes deployment profiles, carbon estimates and optimization recommendations to Operation.

Source: revised manuscript, Deployment Phase Methodology and Deployment Stage Implementation. ReAct foundation: [29].

Component 03

Predictive operation

Anticipate demand and account for which jobs can wait.

Problem and objective. Reactive scaling responds after workload changes. Operation combines predicted demand, carbon estimates and workload urgency to support proactive Kubernetes decisions under SLA constraints.

Approach and implementation

An LSTM processes historical runtime metrics such as CPU utilization, memory, request rates and active pods. The architecture uses a sequence of 12 observations sampled every 30 seconds—a six-minute history—to predict the next 30 seconds.

The Carbon Estimation Engine assesses candidate actions. The Job Priority Engine separates immediate, SLA-sensitive work from delayable background work. High-priority jobs run immediately; medium-priority work can wait only where service quality permits; low-priority batch or maintenance tasks can move to cleaner periods.

The Runtime Decision Engine combines these inputs to recommend Scale Up, Scale Down, Maintain or Delay Jobs.

Evaluation evidence and operational interpretation

On unseen test data from Bitbrains fastStorage, the model achieved R² 0.946 and MAPE 3.28%. The Runtime Decision Engine also generated the intended categories of operational recommendations.

The comparison with Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaling is functional: forecasting, carbon awareness, workload priorities and SLA-aware decisions are additional capabilities. The manuscript does not provide a quantitative comparison of achieved scaling savings or SLA violation rates.

Integration. Predicted demand, workload classifications and carbon-aware recommendations enter the monitoring record. Historical sustainability feedback informs later runtime policies.

Source: revised manuscript, Operation Phase Methodology; Operation Stage Implementation; Runtime Workload Prediction and Operational Intelligence.

Component 04

Sustainability monitoring

Give environmental measurements historical context.

Problem and objective. Current measurements alone cannot establish whether a pipeline stage is behaving unusually. Monitoring constructs stage-specific baselines and identifies potential sustainability regressions across Build, Test and Deploy.

Approach and implementation

A historical repository stores execution duration, processor utilization, infrastructure overhead, estimated energy and emissions, carbon intensity, execution status and timestamps. Statistical Z-scores identify deviations from historical behavior.

Stage-specific, unsupervised Isolation Forest-based models examine multiple sustainability indicators together. The implementation describes Extended Isolation Forest, while the reported evaluation labels its model Isolation Forest. The results below retain that evaluation terminology.

A Sustainability Health Score (SHS), on a 0–100 scale, is described as a consolidated indicator. Prometheus and Grafana support the research system’s telemetry and visualization. The manuscript does not specify the SHS formula or report a benchmark score.

Monitoring experiment and findings

The experiment covered 21 real Jenkins runs, generating 63 stage-level records. Build had the lowest overall impact; Deploy showed the highest mean CPU utilization, energy and emissions; Test was intermediate.

Total estimated energy was 0.001163 kWh, with estimated emissions of 0.000502 kgCO₂e. These are totals for the monitored sample, not savings.

Statistical analysis flagged eight executions: four Build, three Test and one Deploy. Isolation Forest flagged six. The source does not establish overlap or labelled ground truth, so these counts are not accuracy measures or 14 confirmed distinct anomalies.

Integration. Anomaly reports, carbon-efficiency assessments and SHS feed back into build timing, deployment windows, scaling and workload priorities.

Source: revised manuscript, Monitoring Phase Methodology; Monitor Stage Implementation; Sustainability Monitoring and Anomaly Detection. Foundations: [57] [58] [59].

What the combined evidence suggests

Selective execution can reduce redundant CI/CD work with small analysis overhead. Accurate short-term workload forecasts can support proactive operational decisions. Different sustainability patterns across Build, Test and Deploy justify stage-specific baselines.

Statistical and Isolation Forest analysis both produced anomaly flags in the monitored sample. Their results demonstrate feasibility, but do not establish comparative detection accuracy. The integrated framework provides a basis for coordinated sustainability decisions; a measured end-to-end carbon reduction remains to be established.

Source: revised manuscript, CI/CD Optimization Performance; Runtime Workload Prediction and Operational Intelligence; Sustainability Monitoring and Anomaly Detection; Integrated Findings.

Interpretation and validity

Research limitations

  • Estimated energy and emissions. Resource-utilization models can introduce error because direct hardware power measurements were not used.
  • External carbon signals. Decision quality depends on carbon-intensity data availability and accuracy; fallback estimates carry their own uncertainty.
  • Prediction and changing workloads. Models may need retraining. The supplementary Prophet forecast’s approximately 51% MAPE identifies a specific weakness.
  • Scale and generalizability. A single experimental environment and limited samples constrain conclusions. Monitoring covers only 21 pipeline runs.
  • Metrics and ground truth. Sustainability conclusions depend on selected metrics and SHS construction. The manuscript does not report labelled anomaly validation or a reproducible SHS formula.
  • Outcome coverage. Forecast accuracy and execution-time savings do not alone quantify carbon reduction or SLA preservation. Deployment and full-lifecycle environmental benefits need further comparative evaluation.

Source: revised manuscript, Discussion and Threats to Validity, with evaluation boundaries taken from the reported experiments.

Next research steps

Future work

The manuscript proposes improved carbon forecasting and reinforcement-learning-based optimization, alongside broader sustainability indicators such as embodied carbon and infrastructure lifecycle impacts.

Validation across diverse cloud environments and production-scale workloads is needed to test long-term effectiveness, model robustness and the interaction between environmental objectives and service requirements.
